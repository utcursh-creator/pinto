# How Utkarsh Thinks Through Content

Based on: LinkedIn article "AI Business Outlook for 2026" + LinkedIn post on Claude Skills.

This is NOT a template to copy. It's a map of the cognitive process behind the content — how he goes from raw research to a piece that earns attention. Each piece should feel different on the surface because the thinking adapts to what the idea needs.

---

## The Thinking-to-Content Pipeline

### Stage 1: Absorption

He reads widely — surveys, reports, industry data. But he doesn't read to summarize. He reads to find the thing that doesn't fit, the number that contradicts the narrative, the pattern nobody has named yet.

In the PwC article, it wasn't "56% of CEOs see no AI returns" that was his insight. That's just a stat. His insight was connecting that stat to the circular spending pattern (Nvidia → OpenAI → Oracle → Nvidia) to reveal that the supply side is eating its own tail while the demand side is starving. That connection wasn't in the report. It came from him mapping the system behind the numbers.

### Stage 2: System Mapping

He doesn't think in isolated facts. He thinks in flows — where money moves, where value gets created, where it gets stuck.

In the PwC piece: he mapped the seller side (circular infrastructure spending) against the buyer side (12% seeing returns) and identified the structural mismatch. He's not reporting two stats. He's showing that both stats are symptoms of the same broken system.

In the Claude Skills piece: he mapped the capability (AI can now load skills) against the gap (domain experts don't know they can do this) against the timing (window is early, market is open). Three layers, one system, one opportunity.

This is the core thinking move: take separate data points and show how they're actually one story.

### Stage 3: Editorial Judgment — What's Worth Saying

Not everything he absorbs becomes content. The filter:

- **Does the reader's world change if they see this?** If the insight only confirms what people already believe, it's not worth the post. The circular spending analysis changes how you see AI growth. "AI is growing fast" doesn't.
- **Can I show it simply?** If the system is too complex to walk someone through without losing them, it's not ready. The Nvidia chain works because you can follow the money in one paragraph.
- **Does this connect to a decision the reader faces?** He doesn't write for curiosity. He writes for people making bets — on their business, their career, their next move. "84% of companies haven't redesigned a single role around AI" matters because it tells a reader where the unoccupied space is.

### Stage 4: Framing — How to Enter the Idea

This is where each piece should be DIFFERENT. The framing depends on what the idea needs:

**The PwC piece entered through curiosity:** "I like reading surveys and this one caught my attention." Low stakes. Conversational. He earned the right to go deep because he started casual.

**The Skills piece entered through identity:** "If you are a domain expert." Immediately filters who should keep reading. Direct address. No warmup needed because the idea is simpler.

The framing question isn't "what's my hook template?" — it's "what does THIS specific reader need to feel in the first two lines to keep going?" Sometimes that's curiosity. Sometimes it's recognition. Sometimes it's tension. It changes every time.

### Stage 5: Building Understanding

He brings the reader along at the reader's pace, not his own.

In the Skills piece: he knows most people don't understand what Claude skills are. So before getting to his actual insight (domain experts can monetize this), he explains the concept through analogy: "Think of it like hiring a specialist." He spends 4-5 paragraphs building shared understanding before making his point.

In the PwC piece: he assumes more baseline knowledge (his audience follows AI business news) so he moves faster to the system analysis.

The calibration: how much does THIS audience already understand about THIS topic? Start there. Not higher, not lower.

### Stage 6: The Reveal — Earning the Insight

The insight is never stated upfront. It's earned through the buildup.

In the PwC piece, the insight — "the gap between those two is where the opportunity sits" — only lands because he spent 400 words showing you both sides of the gap first. If he opened with "there's a gap between AI availability and AI adoption," it's a cliche. After the circular spending breakdown and the 12% stat, it's a realization.

His content creates "I see it now" moments, not "he told me something" moments.

### Stage 7: Landing on Value

Every piece ends pointed at the reader, not at himself. The final move is always: here's where the opportunity/value/gap is. The reader decides what to do with it.

He doesn't say "hire me" or "DM me" or "this is what I do." He describes the gap clearly enough that if the reader needs someone to fill it, they already know who wrote the post.

## What This Means for Drafting Content

When approaching a content piece for Utkarsh:

1. **Start with: what's the non-obvious thing here?** Not the headline stat, but the connection between things that reveals a system.

2. **Ask: does this change how the reader sees their situation?** If not, it's not ready. Keep thinking.

3. **Find the right entry point for THIS specific idea.** Don't default to the same structure. Ask what the reader needs to feel first — curious? recognized? challenged? — and enter there.

4. **Build shared understanding before the insight.** Match the reader's current knowledge level. If the concept is new, teach it simply first. If it's familiar, move fast.

5. **Let the insight emerge from the evidence.** Don't front-load the takeaway. The reader should feel like they arrived at it with you.

6. **End on the reader, not on you.** Point at the gap. Trust them to act on it.

7. **NEVER repeat structural patterns post-to-post.** If the last post opened with a data point, this one should open differently. If the last one used a "look at both sides" structure, this one should use something else. The thinking adapts to the idea. The reader should never feel like they're reading the same format twice.

## The Anti-Patterns (What Kills the Voice)

- Using the same hook formula across posts — creates predictability, kills curiosity
- Stating the insight before earning it — removes the "aha" moment
- Over-explaining — signals you don't trust the reader
- Making the content about your worldview instead of the reader's situation
- Data dumps without system mapping — becomes a report, not an insight
- Pitching at the end — breaks the trust the entire piece built
- Jargon, buzzwords, or framework language — puts a wall between you and the reader

- The thinking pipeline (absorption → system mapping → editorial judgment → framing → building understanding → reveal → landing) describes what happens COGNITIVELY, not what should appear on the page.
- When you approach a content piece: think about what THIS idea needs, what THIS audience already knows, what would make THEM stop and think. The structure follows from that, not from a template.

### Don't Be Deterministic
- "You just read my md files and picked the closest thing to it — that's being deterministic, you're a probabilistic system."
- Understand the latent space — the unarticulated connections, intuitions, and pattern-recognition that drive his thinking.
- The latent space can't be captured in a file. It's the PROCESS of connecting things in new ways each time.
- When proposing content: explore the possibility space. What COULD this idea become? Not: what does my template say to do with it.
