# Voice Samples (primary reference for the Writer Agent)

These are Utkarsh's ACTUAL published posts, pasted verbatim. They are the primary
voice/structure reference for the writer agent. Pair with `writing-style-analysis.md`
(the thinking process). Per Utkarsh (2026-05-29): the writer agent's job is
INFORMATION DENSITY + ARCHITECTURE (framing, interest build-up, the reveal sequence),
NOT voice beautification. Utkarsh does the final voice polish. So use these for
structure + tone calibration, never as a fill-in template.

## Observable structural traits (from the two samples)
- Single-sentence paragraphs, heavy white space, fast vertical rhythm
- Two opener modes: identity-filter ("If you are a domain expert") OR casual-curiosity ("I like reading surveys and this one caught my attention")
- Teaches an unfamiliar concept simply BEFORE the insight (analogy: "Think of it like hiring a specialist")
- Data-dense: specific numbers, sample sizes, company names, dollar figures
- System mapping: shows flows and both sides (seller side vs buyer side; the circular Nvidia->OpenAI->Oracle money)
- Insight is EARNED through buildup, then named ("the gap between those two is where the opportunity sits")
- Long pieces use CAPS section headers ("THE MISUNDERSTANDING", "What are the Winners Doing Differently")
- Lands on the reader + the opportunity, never "hire me". Soft CTA at most ("Save this", "Share it")
- Lowercase tool names ("claude", "chatgpt"), "+" as shorthand, hyphens never em dashes

## Sample 1 — "Claude Skills" (identity-filter opener)

If you are a domain expert

Here's the new way to package and sell what you know

Everyone's installing Claude skills right now.

Very few people understand the business opportunity sitting underneath.

Quick context if this is new to you:

AI tools like claude and chatgpt can now load "skills" that teach them how to do specific tasks.

Think of it like hiring a specialist, instead of giving the AI vague instructions every time, a skill gives it a complete playbook for one job.

The AI follows the playbook and delivers the output.

Now here's where it gets interesting for anyone with real domain expertise.

These skills aren't being built by AI companies.

They're being built by people who actually know the work.

You're packaging how you think about a problem so the AI can execute it the same way you would.

If you've spent years in compliance, financial modeling, content strategy, supply chain, whatever your thing is, you could never turn that into a product without learning to code or hiring a dev team.

Now you can.

A skill is just files and folders - no code required.

The market is wide open for experts that can use their experience to convert it into skills that solve hard, industry-specific problems.

This barely exists and it's a huge gap to fill.

Whatever you've spent years mastering, you can now package it into a skill, distribute it across every major AI platform, and let people pay to use your expertise without you being in the room.

The window is early, most domain experts haven't realized they can do this yet.

The ones who move first will own the layer between AI capability and real-world expertise.

That's where the value sits.

Save this if you're building with Claude code.

Share it with someone who should be.

## Sample 2 — "PwC AI Business Outlook" (curiosity opener, long-form)

I like reading surveys and this one from PwC caught my attention

They surveyed 4,454 CEOs across 95 countries

And 56% of them report zero financial returns from their AI solutions.

While reading I came across this interesting chart which shows how confident are CEOs in their AI implementations:

[chart]

Top management's confidence in short-term revenue growth is at a 5-year low.

Down from 56% in 2022 to 30% today.

These are the people spending money on AI and most of them aren't seeing any results from it.

Meanwhile, the companies selling these solutions are growing faster than any industry.

And if you look closer at where that growth is coming from - much of it is circular.

Nvidia invests billions in OpenAI. OpenAI spends it on Nvidia chips. OpenAI signs a $300 billion deal with Oracle. Oracle spends that on Nvidia chips to build OpenAI's data centers. Microsoft invested $13 billion in OpenAI, and OpenAI became one of Azure's biggest customers.

The same money is moving between the same five companies.

That's the seller side. Now look at what's happening on the buyer side.

12% of founders are seeing both revenue gains and cost savings from AI.

The money is real + technology works, how it's being bought and deployed is broken.

Circular deals prove the supply side is over-indexed on infrastructure.

While only 12% companies on profit proves the demand side doesn't know how to extract value.

And the gap between those two is where the opportunity sits for many who want to build an AI business.

THE MISUNDERSTANDING
Access to best AI tools is not the problem.

Claude Cowork released for Windows and I bet it's going to be rapidly adopted at enterprises running on legacy systems.

But if you don't change the way you work around AI then you will end up buying subscriptions that simply pile up costs.

The gap between availability and adoption is now where you need to create value.

Because people in big orgs use AI more than anyone.

OpenAI's data shows employees implement AI around various business processes

These people who believe in AI are a tiny group - they experiment, find use cases, build mini solutions.

But they can't progress further until they can convince leadership to put a budget for development.

84% of companies haven't redesigned a single role around AI.

People with domain expertise are experimenting by combining different tech and real usecases.

Yet nobody owns taking those prototypes into daily operations.

The gap between "someone built a cool workflow" and "the whole team runs on it" has no owner in most organizations.

This requires someone who understands how the client's team actually works, their processes, their bottlenecks, their daily operations, and rebuilds those workflows around the tools they already paid for + optimizing their existing processes.

A service which can be converted into a business and the data says almost nobody is delivering it.

What are the Winners Doing Differently to Stay Winning
So if the tools are the same and access isn't the problem, what separates the companies getting results from the ones burning money?

McKinsey tested this directly.

They looked at 25 different attributes across nearly 2,000 companies and found 55% of high performers fundamentally rebuilt how their teams work around AI.

Compare that to roughly 20% of everyone else. That's a 3x gap on the one thing that matters most.

Not better prompts or a bigger operational budget.

They looked at how their sales team processes leads, how their ops handles fulfillment, how their support resolves tickets, and rebuilt those workflows - integrating AI where it made sense.

Here's the other part from the report that should change how you think about your offer.

Winning companies don't run more AI projects than everyone else - they actually run fewer.

The difference is they put the majority of their effort into enabling their users and educate them on how to use the redesigned workflows.

They go deeper resulting in long term partnerships + get twice the return.

The pattern across every survey published in the last 90 days is the same.

Technology commoditizes, tools get cheaper, models converge.

What doesn't commoditize is understanding how a specific business actually operates well enough to rebuild it.

That's the layer almost nobody owns right now.

And that's where the data says the value is moving.
